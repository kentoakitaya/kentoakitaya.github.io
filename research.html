Observations do not always tell us why something happens. More than one explanation may fit the available evidence.

Much of the subsurface cannot be observed directly. Rock samples, groundwater measurements, and movement at the land surface offer partial views of conditions underground. Comparing these sources of evidence can help us test possible causes without necessarily identifying a single answer.

My research to date has focused on groundwater flow, land subsidence, and induced seismicity. Behind these studies is a broader interest in why physical phenomena occur and how much we can learn about their causes from limited observations. I bring together evidence from multiple sources to examine possible explanations, using numerical modelling and uncertainty analysis to test how well those explanations are supported.

Selected Research

Can a nearby well dominate fault pressure when distant wells have injected for decades?

Akitaya et al. (2026), Geophysical Research Letters

A magnitude 5.6 earthquake occurred near Peace River, Alberta, in 2022. Previous studies linked the earthquake sequence to a disposal well about 5 km from the earthquake cluster. Yet other wells roughly 35 km away had been injecting for decades. Could these distant wells also contribute substantially to pressure on the fault?

We used recorded injection histories from 15 wells in a three dimensional pore pressure diffusion model. Rather than assuming one set of hydraulic properties, we varied the permeabilities of the reservoir and fault zones. We then compared the nearby well’s contribution to average pressure on a basement fault region with the combined contribution of the other wells.

The nearby well exceeded the others combined under some hydraulic conditions, but not all. Faster pressure transmission made the long histories of distant injection more important. This result shows why proximity alone cannot reliably determine whether a nearby well dominates fault pressure loading.

Why does land subsidence continue despite recovering groundwater levels?

Akitaya and Aichi (2023), Hydrogeology Journal

In Kawajima, Japan, land subsidence continued despite a long term recovery in groundwater levels. We investigated this apparent contradiction using groundwater records, hydrogeological observations, and numerical modelling.

The evidence indicated that seasonal groundwater extraction for agriculture was a major contributor. Numerical modelling helped explain how repeated groundwater drawdowns could produce progressive inelastic compaction.

Why a good fit is not enough

Akitaya and Aichi (2024), Water

Different combinations of subsurface properties can reproduce the same observed land subsidence. However, conventional data assimilation methods may converge prematurely, losing model diversity and creating a misleading impression of well-constrained parameters.

We addressed this challenge using evolutionary data assimilation (EDA) with fitness sharing. By adjusting the niche radius, we examined the tradeoff between fitting observations and preserving diverse parameter sets. The method maintained model diversity even after 1,000 assimilation cycles in a highly nonlinear land subsidence model.

We also recognized that diverse parameter estimates do not necessarily lead to reliable probabilistic predictions. We therefore combined EDA with ensemble model output statistics (EMOS) to correct predictive bias and dispersion.

The central contribution is to distinguish three goals that are often treated together. These goals are reproducing observations, characterizing parameter uncertainty, and quantifying predictive uncertainty.
